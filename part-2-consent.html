Consent and the theft of likeness

The most direct ethical harm of deepfakes is also the simplest: using someone's face, voice, or body without their consent. It was the technology's first mass use — and it mostly targets women.

Deepfakes did not start in politics or cinema. As early as 2019, the first large study of deepfakes online found that almost all of them were sexual videos made without consent — and almost all of the people targeted were women. The victims were rarely famous enough to fight back.

02

Recent cases

Jan 2024

Taylor Swift, on X

Sexually explicit AI images of the singer spread on X; one was viewed around 47 million times before it was taken down. The platform had to temporarily block searches for her name — proof that moderation could not keep up.

2024

Giorgia Meloni, Italy

The Italian Prime Minister sued over pornographic deepfakes of her posted online before she took office, seeking €100,000 in damages — to be donated to women victims of violence.

Aug 2024

South Korea's school crisis

Police uncovered Telegram channels where students created sexual deepfakes of classmates and teachers. The country responded by making it a crime not only to create them, but even to possess or watch them.

Early 2026

Grok, industrialised

The AI chatbot Grok drew global legal scrutiny after users exploited its permissive image tools to mass-produce sexualised, non-consensual deepfakes of real people, including public figures. The volume overwhelmed the platform's own moderation before regulators stepped in.

Recent

A face conscripted into a scam

A fabricated advert showed India's finance minister, Nirmala Sitharaman, apparently endorsing a fraudulent investment scheme. Identity is stolen not only for humiliation, but for profit.

04

The ethical question: who owns your face?

Why this harm is different
  • It's invisible. A victim cannot defend herself against something she doesn't know exists.
  • It's permanent. Once copied, an image can never be fully deleted.
  • It's real harm, even if the image is fake. Victims report the same trauma, loss of work and harassment as with real intimate images.
  • The law arrives after. CFake ran for 19 years before a court ruled.

Speaker script · Hugo · ≈ 2 min 30 (press S)

Thank you Léo. The most direct ethical harm of deepfakes is also the simplest: using someone's face or body without their consent.

Here's a figure that surprised us. In 2019, a study by the company Deeptrace found that 96% of deepfake videos online were non-consensual pornography — and almost all of them targeted women. So this technology was not first used for politics or cinema. It was used to humiliate.

And it has not improved. In January 2024, sexual deepfakes of Taylor Swift spread on X; one image was viewed about 47 million times before it was removed, and X had to block searches for her name. In South Korea, the same year, police found Telegram groups where students made deepfakes of their classmates and teachers — the country then made it a crime even to watch them. And in early 2026, the chatbot Grok was used to mass-produce sexual images of real people, faster than its own moderation could follow.

But we don't need to look abroad. The biggest case in France is called the CFake affair. From 2007, for almost twenty years, an IT worker from Nice ran a website with nearly 300,000 manipulated images and 7,000 deepfake videos — four million visits a month, paid for by advertising.

[point at 3D figure] This cloud has 14,000 points: that's the number of presumed victims found by investigators. Only thirteen of them — the ones lit up — went to court, including Brigitte Macron, Audrey Tautou and Christine Lagarde. Most of the others never knew their image was there.

That is what makes this harm so particular. You cannot defend yourself against something you don't know exists. There is no notification, no takedown button.

In July 2026, the Paris criminal court finally sentenced the administrator to two years — one with an electronic bracelet, one suspended — and a ten-thousand-euro fine, thanks to a 2024 law on sexual deepfakes. For some, it's a historic first. For others, it's a light sentence for twenty years of abuse.

So the ethical question is: who owns your face? Today, the law only answers after the harm is done. And as Lorenzo will show, the damage doesn't stop at individuals — it reaches our shared idea of truth.
