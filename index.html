When the camera lies

What are the ethical implications of deepfake technology in digital media? Generative AI can now clone a face, steal a voice, and fabricate a conversation that never happened. The same tool can be a joke, a work of art — or a weapon.

By Léo Michard, Hugo Deschamps, Lorenzo Sasia & Tim Marsat

Exhibit A: “Macron, live on TikTok”

● LIVE AI FACE-SWAP · NOT MACRON
Real-time face-swap by digital artist SYSTAIME · looped, muted

It looks like the French President chatting with viewers on a TikTok live. It is someone else, wearing Emmanuel Macron's face like a mask — in real time.

This is the point where deepfakes stopped being a special effect. A decade ago, swapping a face required a film studio and weeks of work. Today, face-swap software runs live on a gaming laptop, fast enough to fool a webcam, a livestream — or, as Lorenzo will show, a video call with your boss.

Macron himself has played with it. In February 2025, just before hosting the AI Action Summit in Paris, he posted a montage of deepfakes of himself — new hairstyles, dance routines, a scene from a French comedy film — to promote the event. It was funny, it went viral, and it quietly asked the question at the centre of this presentation: if a head of state treats deepfakes as entertainment, where exactly is the ethical line?

02

A technology that outran its guardrails

Deepfakes are synthetic images, audio, or video generated by AI to convincingly depict people saying or doing things they never did.

The name mixes deep learning and fake. A neural network is trained on thousands of images of a face until it can redraw it from any angle, with any expression — which is what Figure 1 shows: a face reduced to a cloud of data points that can be reshaped at will, then put back together.

Voice cloning in particular has crossed what researchers call an "indistinguishable threshold": a few seconds of audio is now enough to produce a synthetic voice with natural intonation, rhythm, and even breathing. That accessibility is the ethical hinge of the whole debate — deepfakes are no longer confined to nation-state actors or film studios, which changes who can be harmed, and how often.

03

Same tool, two uses: where is the line?

The technology itself is neutral. What makes a deepfake ethical or unethical is not the algorithm, but two questions: did the person consent? and does the audience know it's synthetic?

WITH CONSENT & DISCLOSURE

  • Actor Val Kilmer, who lost his voice to throat cancer, had it rebuilt by AI for Top Gun: Maverick (2022).
  • Macron's self-deepfakes (2025): his own face, clearly labelled as a joke.
  • Parody and satire — like the SYSTAIME video — when it is obvious it's fake.

WITHOUT CONSENT OR DISCLOSURE

  • Sexual deepfakes of real people — the subject of Part 2.
  • Fake political speeches before an election — Part 3.
  • A cloned CFO on a video call, asking for $25 million — Part 3.

Even the "good" column is not simple. A parody shared without its caption becomes disinformation. A head of state normalising deepfakes as a joke makes it harder to explain that the same tool destroys lives. That tension is what the next three parts explore.

Speaker script · Léo · ≈ 2 min 30 (press S)

[video looping] Good morning everyone. Before we start, look at this video. That's Emmanuel Macron, apparently live on TikTok, chatting with his viewers. Except… it isn't Macron. It's a face-swap made by the French digital artist Systaime: a real person, in real time, wearing the President's face like a mask.

This is what we call a deepfake: synthetic images, audio or video generated by artificial intelligence to show people saying or doing things they never did. The word mixes "deep learning" and "fake". [point at 3D figure] A neural network studies thousands of pictures of a face until it can redraw it from any angle — which is what this figure represents: a face turned into data, that can be reshaped at will.

Why does it matter now? Because it has become cheap and easy. The number of deepfakes online went from about half a million in 2023 to almost eight million in 2025. And for voices, a few seconds of audio is enough to clone someone — including their breathing.

Interestingly, Macron himself played with this. In February 2025, just before the AI Action Summit in Paris, he posted a montage of deepfakes of himself — with a mullet, dancing, even inside a French comedy film. It was funny, and it got millions of views. But it raises our central question: if a president treats deepfakes as a joke, how do we explain that the same technology can destroy lives?

Because the technology is neutral. The same tool that gave the actor Val Kilmer his voice back in Top Gun: Maverick, after throat cancer, can also fake a confession, a scandal, or a pornographic video. So our argument is this: the ethical line is not the technology. It is two things — consent, and disclosure. Did the person agree? And does the audience know it's fake?

We'll explore this in three steps. Hugo will talk about consent and the victims, with a major French case. Lorenzo will show how deepfakes attack trust, in elections and in companies. And Tim will present the answers: the law, and the technology that could prove what is real. Hugo, over to you.
