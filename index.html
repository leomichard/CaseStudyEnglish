When the camera lies

What are the ethical implications of deepfake technology in digital media? Generative AI can now clone a face, steal a voice, and fabricate a conversation that never happened. The same tool can be a joke, a work of art, or a weapon.

01

Exhibit A: “Macron, live on TikTok”

● LIVE AI FACE-SWAP · NOT MACRON
Real-time face-swap by digital artist SYSTAIME · looped, muted

It looks like the French President chatting with viewers on a TikTok live. It is someone else, wearing Emmanuel Macron's face like a mask, in real time.

This shows how far deepfakes have come. A decade ago, swapping a face required a film studio and weeks of work. Today, face-swap software runs live on a gaming laptop, fast enough to fool a webcam, a livestream or, as Lorenzo will show, a video call with your boss.

Macron himself has played with it. In February 2025, just before hosting the AI Action Summit in Paris, he posted a montage of deepfakes of himself (new hairstyles, dance routines, a scene from a French comedy film) to promote the event. It was funny and it went viral, but it also raises the question at the centre of this presentation: if a head of state treats deepfakes as entertainment, where exactly is the ethical line?

02

A technology anyone can use

Deepfakes are synthetic images, audio, or video generated by AI to convincingly depict people saying or doing things they never did.

The name mixes deep learning and fake. A neural network is trained on thousands of images of a face until it can redraw it from any angle, with any expression. That is what Figure 1 shows: a face reduced to a cloud of data points that can be reshaped at will, then put back together.

Voice cloning in particular has crossed what researchers call an "indistinguishable threshold": a few seconds of audio is now enough to produce a synthetic voice with natural intonation, rhythm, and even breathing. This is why the debate matters: deepfakes are no longer reserved for governments or film studios, so anyone can now be a target.

03

Same tool, two uses: where is the line?

The technology itself is neutral. What makes a deepfake ethical or unethical is not the algorithm, but two questions: did the person consent? and does the audience know it's synthetic?

WITH CONSENT & DISCLOSURE

  • Actor Val Kilmer, who lost his voice to throat cancer, had it rebuilt by AI for Top Gun: Maverick (2022).
  • Macron's self-deepfakes (2025): his own face, clearly labelled as a joke.
  • Parody and satire, like the SYSTAIME video, when it is obvious it's fake.

WITHOUT CONSENT OR DISCLOSURE

  • Sexual deepfakes of real people (Part 2).
  • Fake political speeches before an election (Part 3).
  • A cloned CFO on a video call, asking for $25 million (Part 3).

Even the "good" column is not simple. A parody shared without its caption becomes disinformation. A head of state normalising deepfakes as a joke makes it harder to explain that the same tool destroys lives. That tension is what the next three parts explore.
