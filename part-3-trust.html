01

Deepfakes at the ballot box

Mar 2022

Zelensky "surrenders"

Weeks into the Russian invasion, a crude deepfake of Ukraine's president telling his soldiers to lay down their arms was pushed onto a hacked news site. It was quickly debunked — an early warning of deepfakes as a weapon of war.

Sep 2023

Slovakia: the audio two days before the vote

A fake recording of liberal leader Michal Šimečka appearing to discuss rigging the election spread during the legal pre-election silence, when media and candidates could not respond. His party lost. Nobody can prove the fake changed the result — and that uncertainty is the problem.

Jan 2024

"Biden" tells voters to stay home

Thousands of New Hampshire voters got a robocall with a cloned Joe Biden voice telling them not to vote in the primary. The US regulator (FCC) banned AI voices in robocalls and fined the consultant behind it $6 million.

2024

India's election, by WhatsApp

Fabricated clips of major leaders, including Narendra Modi and Rahul Gandhi, spread widely on WhatsApp and YouTube — used as a deliberate tool for narrative control, not isolated pranks.

02

The liar's dividend

The deepest cost isn't any single fake clip. It's that once fakes are common knowledge, real footage becomes deniable too.

A politician caught on a genuine recording can simply say "it's AI". Genuine evidence gets dismissed as "probably fake", which corrodes public trust in all media — not just in the fakes. Deepfakes don't only make us believe false things; they make us doubt true ones. And as Figure 3 shows, the correction always starts late and travels slower than the lie.

Feb 2024

A $25 million video call

A finance employee at the Hong Kong office of engineering firm Arup joined what looked like a routine call with the company's CFO and colleagues. Every face on the call was a deepfake. He authorised the transfers.

May 2024

The impersonation that almost worked

Scammers used a public photo of WPP CEO Mark Read and a voice clone on a Teams call. Staff asked a question the impersonator couldn't answer — the fraud collapsed before any money moved.

2025

A $15 identity, verified

A service called OnlyFake generated synthetic passports realistic enough to pass the automated ID checks of a major crypto exchange — for the price of a takeaway meal.

Most of the $3.7 billion was lost in 2025 and the first half of 2026 alone, and only publicly reported cases are counted — the true figure is almost certainly higher.

Speaker script · Lorenzo · ≈ 2 min 30 (press S)

Thanks Hugo. Deepfakes don't only harm individuals. They can distort the information a whole country uses to decide how to vote.

Take Slovakia, September 2023. Two days before the election, a fake audio recording spread online, where the leader of the liberal party seemed to discuss rigging the vote. It came out during the legal silence period, when politicians and journalists can't respond. His party lost. We can't prove the fake changed the result — but that uncertainty is exactly the problem.

In January 2024 in the US, thousands of voters in New Hampshire got a phone call from "Joe Biden", telling them not to vote. It was a cloned voice. The regulator reacted by banning AI voices in robocalls, and fined the man behind it six million dollars. In India too, fake clips of Modi and Rahul Gandhi circulated massively on WhatsApp.

[point at 3D figure] This animation shows why it's so dangerous. The fake, in red, starts from one point and spreads through the network. The fact-check, in blue, starts later and moves slower. By the time it arrives, almost everyone has seen the lie.

But the deepest cost is what researchers call the "liar's dividend". Once everyone knows fakes exist, anyone can dismiss real evidence as "probably AI". So deepfakes don't only make us believe false things — they make us doubt true ones.

And this loss of trust has a price. In February 2024, an employee of the engineering company Arup in Hong Kong joined a video call with his financial director and several colleagues. Every single person on that call was a deepfake. He transferred 25 million dollars. A few months later, criminals tried the same trick on the boss of WPP, the world's biggest advertising group — but an employee asked a question the fake couldn't answer, and the scam failed.

In total, deepfake fraud has cost around 3.7 billion dollars since 2020, most of it in the last eighteen months.

So, ethically: who is responsible? The creator, the platform that spreads it, or the victim who believed it? Tim will now present the answers.
