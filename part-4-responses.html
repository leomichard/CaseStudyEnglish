01

The law is catching up

Feb 2024 · European Union
Digital Services Act fully in force

Platforms operating in Europe must remove illegal content, including non-consensual deepfakes, once they are notified.

May 2024 · France
Sexual deepfakes become a specific crime

Articles 226-8 and 226-8-1 of the penal code: up to 3 years and €75,000 when shared online. The law that convicted the CFake administrator.

May 2025 · United States
TAKE IT DOWN Act

Publishing non-consensual intimate images, real or AI-generated, becomes a federal crime; platforms must remove them within 48 hours of a request.

2025 · Denmark
Copyright over your own face

Denmark proposed giving every citizen a form of copyright over their face, voice and body, so they can demand the removal of deepfakes of themselves.

Aug 2026 · European Union
AI Act transparency rules

Deepfakes must be clearly labelled as made or changed by AI. Léo's "disclosure" principle becomes law.

02

Where embedded systems come in

The hardware side of the problem

Most of the deepfake debate is about software: the models that create fakes and the algorithms that detect them. But these fixes only work if the original image can be trusted, and that depends on the hardware.

  • Provenance at capture. Signing a photo with cryptography the moment the sensor captures it is the idea behind the C2PA "Content Credentials" standard, already used in some Leica, Sony and Nikon cameras. It needs a secure chip inside the camera itself.
  • Liveness detection. Telling a real face from a screen or a mask needs dedicated hardware, like the depth and infrared sensors in Face ID, because a simple 2D image is easy to fake.
  • On-device detection. With a detection chip inside the device, a video call can be checked in real time, without sending data to the cloud.
  • Roots of trust. Secure chips such as TPMs are the base of the whole chain. If the chip is hacked, none of its signatures can be trusted.

In short, detecting deepfakes is a software problem, but proving that a photo is real is an embedded-systems problem.

03

No single fix

None of the cases we presented were stopped by technology alone. The WPP scam was stopped by a human asking a question the fake could not answer. The Arup fraud was not. To protect people, we need four things:

Laws
that punish and force removal
Hardware
that proves what is real
Platforms
that take responsibility
People
who know seeing ≠ believing

Speaker script · Tim · ≈ 2 min 30 (press S)

Thank you Lorenzo. So, what can we do? We think the answer has two sides: the law, and the technology.

First, the law is finally catching up. In France, since May 2024, sharing a sexual deepfake of someone without consent is a specific crime. That's the law that convicted the CFake administrator. In Europe, the Digital Services Act forces platforms to remove illegal content once notified, and since August 2026 the AI Act requires deepfakes to be clearly labelled as artificial. In the United States, the Take It Down Act, signed in 2025, forces platforms to remove intimate deepfakes within 48 hours. And Denmark proposed something radical: giving every citizen a kind of copyright over their own face and voice.

But laws act after the damage. That's where our field, embedded systems, comes in. Detecting deepfakes is a software arms race, and detectors often lose. So the better question is not "is this fake?" but "can we prove this is real?"

[point at 3D figure] This chain shows the idea. It starts at the camera sensor. A secure chip inside the camera signs the image the instant it's captured. That's the C2PA standard, already in some Leica, Sony and Nikon cameras. Each edit adds a signed step. If someone modifies the image without signing (watch the red link), the chain breaks, and the viewer knows it can't be trusted.

Other hardware helps too: depth and infrared sensors, like Face ID, can tell a real face from a screen or a mask. And edge-AI chips can flag a fake live, during a video call, without sending anything to the cloud.

But none of this works alone. Remember WPP: it wasn't an algorithm that stopped the scam, it was a human asking the right question.

So, to conclude: deepfakes are not unethical by nature. Léo showed they can even be art. They become unethical without consent and without disclosure. And the answer must combine laws that punish, hardware that proves, platforms that take responsibility, and citizens who learn that seeing is no longer believing.
